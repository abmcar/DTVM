\section{Introduction}

The execution layer of a blockchain information system is, in essence, a
replicated consensus state machine: every node on the consensus path must
produce bit-identical results for the same transaction. The EVM is the most
widely deployed smart-contract execution environment, and its just-in-time
(JIT) compiler is progressively replacing the interpreter in mainstream
clients, becoming a core component on the consensus-critical path. Peephole
rewrite rules in the JIT act directly on the machine-code generation stage,
so their correctness bears directly on the determinism of on-chain
execution.

If the equivalence judgment of a peephole rewrite rule is wrong and the
rule is deployed on only some clients, the nodes may produce inconsistent
execution results and trigger a chain fork---one of the most expensive
failure modes in a blockchain information system. Existing formal
verification work on EVM peephole rewrites focuses mostly on the bytecode
block-level abstraction. The machine-level patterns emitted by a JIT
compiler during code generation---especially the multi-word carry chains
that arise when the 256-bit word length is lowered onto 64-bit
hardware---sit at an adjacent but different abstraction layer, and an
end-to-end automatically verifiable rule set with a concrete deployment
path is still missing.

The root cause of this gap is how intermediate representations model the
bit-level side-products of arithmetic (such as the carry bit). In a
general-purpose intermediate representation such as LLVM IR, the basic
arithmetic operations are defined only on $N$-bit integers; the carry bit
must be retrieved indirectly through an intrinsic that returns a
multi-return tuple of \{result, carry\}. The bit-level side-product is not
a native type. Rules that depend on such side-products can therefore only
be expressed through indirect structures in Alive~\cite{alive} and similar
automatic equivalence-checking tools; the bottleneck is not the capacity
of the SMT solver~\cite{demoura2008z3} but the expressiveness of the IR
and its pattern matching. For a consensus-critical JIT, if a rule class
cannot be verified automatically, the engineer is left with three
choices: drop the rule, ship it without formal verification, or rely on a
hand-written paper proof. Each choice sacrifices performance, endangers
consensus, or fails to scale. The result is that such rules are
systematically excluded from the optimization surface.

The direction taken in this paper is the following: \emph{without
modifying the solver and without loosening the differential-test
threshold, expand the coverage of SMT-verifiable peephole rules by
extending the IR with first-class bit-vector operators.} In dMIR, the
bit-level patterns that previously required an indirect multi-return
encoding become first-class operators, so the corresponding rules fall
naturally within the decidable fragment of the Z3 bit-vector equivalence
check.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An automatically verifiable peephole system through
    IR-side extension.} Bit-level side-products that a general-purpose IR
    can only carry through a multi-return tuple become first-class
    bit-vector operators of dMIR. Without modifying the solver and
    without loosening the differential-test threshold, the IR extension
    moves a rule class that previously sat outside the Alive2
    automatic equivalence-verification framework into the decidable
    fragment of the Z3 bit-vector equivalence
    check~\cite{demoura2008z3}. A two-tier rewrite system is built on
    \textbf{70} dMIR production rules (4 of which rely on the
    first-class modeling and cannot be expressed as a single peephole
    rule on general IR) together with \textbf{13} rules at the x86
    code-generation layer (covered by differential tests; no separate
    SMT check).
  \item \textbf{Capacity, admission rate, and production-rule coverage
    of the synthesis pipeline.} A capacity run evaluates \textbf{853}
    candidates under the Z3 admission check (\textbf{98.7\%} on
    algebraic enumeration, \textbf{21.8\%} on carry templates); a
    separate coverage run rediscovers \textbf{70/70} production rule
    names under canonical-LHS comparison (\S\ref{sec:evaluation}). The
    two channels are operationally separate from production rule
    maintenance, but the search space contains the production set.
  \item \textbf{End-to-end performance and correctness.} Across the
    \textbf{27} EVM benchmarks of evmone-bench, the geometric-mean
    speedup is \textbf{+7.24\%} (95\% confidence interval
    [+2.59\%, +12.11\%], lower bound significantly greater than zero),
    with a peak of \textbf{+25.79\%}; \textbf{5884} differential tests
    agree byte-for-byte on state and gas between the rules-enabled and
    rules-disabled sides (DTVM self-A/B, same engine).
\end{enumerate}

Relative to the EVM bytecode block-level formal rule set of
Albert et al.~\cite{verified_evm_cav23} at CAV 2023, the rule set in this
paper operates at the JIT intermediate representation and machine-code
layers that the bytecode abstraction cannot reach (quantitative comparison
in \S\ref{sec:related}).
