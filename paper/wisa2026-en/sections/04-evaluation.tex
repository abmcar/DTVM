\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This section evaluates the two-tier peephole rewrite system along three
axes: the output of the rule synthesis pipeline against the
general-purpose IR checker (\S4.1), end-to-end execution performance
(\S4.2), and correctness with rule coverage (\S4.3).

\subsection{Rule Synthesis Output Against the General-Purpose IR}

Table~\ref{tab:synth-alive2} combines the admission results from the
synthesis pipeline of \S\ref{sec:synthesis} with the verification
ability of Alive2 on the same rule groups.

\input{tables/tab4-1-synth-alive2}

Z3 solves a typical 256-bit bit-vector peephole equivalence proposition
in about 60\,ms on average; all 70 dMIR rule queries finish within
seconds, with no timeout or undecidable outcome, showing that the 256-bit
width is not a bottleneck for the solver's capacity. From the capacity
run, 10 typical U256 rules were sampled at random and compared against
Alive2: 6 of them do not involve a multi-return carry structure
and Alive2 discharges them directly; the other 4 involve
\texttt{adc}/\texttt{sbb} multi-return structures, for which Alive2
either reports a type mismatch or times out (the mechanism is of the same
type as Table~\ref{tab:cannot-express} in \S\ref{sec:expressiveness},
while these 4 are an independent sample from the production rule set),
whereas Z3 discharges all of them directly under the dMIR bit-vector
semantics, confirming the analysis of the IR expressiveness bottleneck in
\S\ref{sec:expressiveness}.

\paragraph{Production rule coverage.} Using the canonical-LHS
methodology of \S\ref{sec:synthesis}, the coverage run produces
\textbf{1966} Z3-verified rewrite rules: \textbf{1856} from the
extended algebraic enumerator and \textbf{110} from pattern-template
instantiation. The pattern-template path starts from 2964 candidates,
which Z3 verification and deduplication against the enumerator output
and the existing rule file reduce to the final 110. On the 70
hand-designed production rules, the run matches \textbf{69/69} unique
canonical patterns---one commutative-twin pair, $\texttt{or}(x,y)$
versus $\texttt{or}(y,x)$, shares a single canonical representative
that both production names match---equivalently \textbf{70/70}
production rule names; the false-miss rate against the production set
is therefore \textbf{0/70}.

\paragraph{Scope of the coverage claim.} Two boundaries should be
stated clearly. First, the result measures coverage of the
\emph{existing} production rule set; the extended enumerator's
left-hand-side operator tops and constant pool were broadened in light
of an earlier 0/70 baseline that exposed mechanical gaps (missing tops
such as \texttt{not}/\texttt{select}/\texttt{shl}/\texttt{ushr}/%
\texttt{sshr} as left-hand-side roots, missing small constants, and
structural-equality dedup of forms such as $\texttt{and}(x,x)$).
Reaching 70/70 therefore demonstrates that the production rules lie
inside an enumerable and Z3-discharged search space; it does not
demonstrate blind recall of an unseen target. Second, rediscovery is
defined on canonical left-hand sides; the synthesized right-hand side
is verified equivalent to its own left-hand side by Z3 but is not
required to be syntactically identical to the production rule's
right-hand side. Together with the admission rate above, this result
shows the search space is broad enough to contain every hand-designed
rule, while 1966 of its members pass Z3 verification.

\subsection{End-to-End Performance}

\paragraph{Experimental setup and noise control.} The baseline is
upstream main (already containing 2 hard-coded x86 peephole rules); the
enabled side adds the 83 new rules (70 dMIR + 13 x86). Both run under
evmone-bench, 20 repetitions each, with bootstrap-estimated geometric-mean
95\% CIs ($B{=}10\,000$). The process is pinned with \texttt{taskset -c
2-3} at \texttt{nice -5}, with CPU turbo disabled and load average
$<\!0.5$; benchmarks with CV $\geq 3\%$ are rerun with 30 repetitions.

\input{figures/fig4-speedup}

\input{tables/tab4-2-attribution}

Across the 27 benchmarks (Figure~\ref{fig:speedup}), the enabled rule
set delivers a geometric-mean speedup of \textbf{+7.24\%} (95\% CI
[+2.59\%, +12.11\%]), peaking at \textbf{+25.79\%} on
\texttt{JUMPDEST\_n0/empty}. \texttt{sha1\_shifts/5311} and
\texttt{sha1\_divs/5311} reach +24.81\% and +23.51\%; empty-input sha1
holds at +19.59\%--+20.24\%; \texttt{blake2b\_huff} gains
+10.41\%--+11.54\%. Per-family attribution appears in
Table~\ref{tab:family-attribution} (hit rate = fraction of executed
instructions matched). U256 rules reach $\sim$100\% on
\texttt{mg\_mload}, 33.8\% on \texttt{weierstrudel}, 25.3\% on
\texttt{snailtracer}; the sha1/\texttt{blake2b} speedups come from the
x86 branch/compare-merge rules. Hit rate and speedup are not
proportional---\texttt{mg\_mload} matches almost every instruction with
no-op elimination but per-rewrite saving is small. The two layers are
complementary: multi-word rules concentrate gains on U256-heavy
contracts, while x86 cleanup gives broader ordinary gains.

\paragraph{Discussion of the regressed surface.} Five benchmarks regress
beyond $-2\%$: the 4 \texttt{memory\_grow\_mstore} variants by 7.74\%--%
8.85\% and \texttt{weierstrudel/1} by 2.71\% (CV $<2\%$ on both sides).
The 4 \texttt{mstore} variants cluster on the same write path
(\S\ref{sec:limitations}, item 7), while the matched \texttt{mload}
variants accelerate by +6.03\%--+7.26\%; the regressions appear only in
a $\sim$0.7\,ns/op microbenchmark, not on the macro sha1/\texttt{blake2b}
gains.

\paragraph{Compilation overhead.} Across 776 JIT unit-test modules, the
median single-module compile time is 0.45\,ms and the 95th percentile is
0.87\,ms, covering the full loading--analysis--codegen pipeline; dMIR
peephole rewriting is one local pass within it. The 0.87\,ms is an
upper bound on the whole pipeline, not the incremental cost of the
rewrite pass; per-pass attribution is future work
(\S\ref{sec:limitations}, item 6).

\subsection{Correctness and Rule Coverage}
\label{sec:correctness}

\input{tables/tab4-3-correctness}

The enabled side and the baseline side each run four differential test
categories (Table~\ref{tab:correctness}); the two sides agree
item-by-item on state and gas, for a cumulative
\textbf{5884}/\textbf{5884}. A pass on the baseline side shows that the
test suite itself is correct; a pass on the enabled side shows that the
83 new rules introduce no correctness regression. The multipass JIT unit
tests contain 223 independent Cancun cases; the interpreter run list
contains 226 lines, reducing to 215 after the gtest deduplication;
\texttt{evmone-statetest} on Cancun covers 101 suites (with
\texttt{-k fork\_Cancun} filtering out the Prague fork that DTVM does
not yet support) and runs independently in both the multipass JIT mode
and the interpreter mode. The cross-check between the two modes on the
same test matrix produces identical outcomes, which is direct evidence
of EVM consensus consistency.

Compared with the CAV 2023 rule set of
Albert et al.~\cite{verified_evm_cav23}, 7 of the 83 rules in this paper
overlap: \texttt{dmir\_add\_zero}, \texttt{dmir\_mul\_one\_rhs},
\texttt{dmir\_mul\_zero\_rhs}, \texttt{dmir\_or\_zero},
\texttt{dmir\_double\_not}, \texttt{dmir\_sub\_self}, and
\texttt{dmir\_and\_factor\_lhs}, which correspond respectively to the
CAV'23 rules \texttt{optimize\_add\_zero}, \texttt{optimize\_mul\_one},
\texttt{optimize\_mul\_zero}, \texttt{optimize\_or\_zero},
\texttt{optimize\_not\_not}, \texttt{optimize\_sub\_x\_x}, and
\texttt{optimize\_and\_and\_l}. All 7 are general bit-vector algebraic
identities ($\texttt{add}(x,0) \to x$, $\texttt{mul}(x,0) \to 0$,
$\texttt{not}(\texttt{not}(x)) \to x$, and so on) that appear in almost
every SMT-verifiable peephole effort. The remaining 76 rules cover dMIR
algebraic simplification and multi-word cleanup (63 rules) and x86
register/branch/flag cleanup (13 rules). These transformations live at
abstraction layers that the bytecode layer cannot reach directly---which
is precisely the point that the two rule sets target different
abstraction layers.

$\sim$0.5\,s total (offline, distinct from the runtime compilation
latency of \S4.2).
