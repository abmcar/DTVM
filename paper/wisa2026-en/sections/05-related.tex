\section{Related Work and Discussion}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Verifiable Peephole Optimization Frameworks}

This paper positions its work along three axes: the abstraction layer it
targets, the verification method, and carry-chain coverage. The
synthesis-then-validation paradigm dates back to Denali by Joshi et
al.~\cite{denali_pldi02} and the peephole superoptimizer of Bansal and
Aiken~\cite{bansal_aiken_asplos06}; STOKE~\cite{stoke} and Stratified
Synthesis~\cite{stratified} explored stochastic and learning-based
alternatives, while equality saturation as embodied by
egg~\cite{egg} offers a non-SMT route. SMT-based verifiable peephole
optimization is exemplified by Alive~\cite{alive}, its successor
Alive2~\cite{alive2}, Souper~\cite{souper}, and---in the verified-compiler
setting---PEEK for CompCert by Mullen et al.~\cite{peek}. Pruning
techniques such as dataflow-based filtering~\cite{dataflow_pruning}
further accelerate this pipeline. This paper follows the same
``synthesize candidates, then admit through SMT equivalence'' paradigm,
but changes the IR on which the check is performed from general-purpose
LLVM IR to the dMIR of the EVM JIT. The PLDI 2015 Alive paper already
points out that the multi-return encoding of carry and overflow flags in
LLVM limits the expressiveness of peephole verification---this
observation is the starting point of the present paper, not a new
finding.

LeanMLIR by Bhat et al.\ at ITP 2024~\cite{leanmlir} formalizes in
Lean~4 the idea of ``parameterizing the peephole verification framework
over the IR'', and its positioning is complementary to ours: LeanMLIR
focuses on the generality of the proof framework across IRs, whereas
this paper focuses on the instantiated rule set and end-to-end
integration on a concrete IR.

Hydra by Mukherjee and Regehr at OOPSLA 2024~\cite{hydra} automatically
discovers peephole rules that are independent of the input bit width.
Like Alive2, however, it builds on LLVM IR, and therefore inherits the
expressiveness limit on multi-word carry chains described in
\S\ref{sec:problem}; the present paper offers one engineering solution
by changing the underlying IR. The FMBC 2020 work of Schett and
Nagele~\cite{fmbc20_evm_peephole} uses a similar offline
enumeration-plus-SMT pipeline to automatically generate about 993 rules
for EVM \emph{bytecode}; the equivalence guarantee is on par, and the
difference is in the abstraction layer: the rule set of this paper acts
on the JIT code-generation path and can exploit both structured
matching on dMIR and mechanical cleanup on x86, while the gap between
993 and 83 rules reflects the difference in abstraction layer and
coverage strategy. Adopting the bytecode-layer rule set as a JIT
pre-processing stage and measuring the residual speedup after
composition with the rules of this paper (an orthogonal-composition
evaluation) is a separate design choice: the current DTVM production
pipeline does not take the bytecode pre-processing path, so the present
paper does not cover this combination and leaves it for future work.

The \texttt{adc}/\texttt{sbb} carry chain of multi-word integers is the
standard implementation form used by GMP and libsecp256k1 on 64-bit
hardware, and maps to the Intel ADCX/ADOX instructions; a carry chain of
four 64-bit limbs is the standard lowered shape of EVM 256-bit
arithmetic on 64-bit hardware.

\subsection{Verification Work on the Consensus-Critical Path}

Albert et al.\ at CAV 2023~\cite{verified_evm_cav23} complete a Coq
formal proof for an AOT optimization rule set on EVM bytecode blocks.
Their positioning is bytecode-block level, offline-proof based, and
built on general bit-vector identities; the positioning of this paper is
at the JIT IR and machine-code layers, with SMT admission at rule
synthesis time, and covers EVM-specific multi-word carry chains. The
scales are not comparable: the 14 rules of
Albert\footnote{The public \texttt{forves} artifact accompanying~\cite{verified_evm_cav23}
(Coq source \texttt{optimizations.v}, 14 rules) is used as the
comparison baseline; the CAV 2023 paper text discusses 15 rules,
and the extended-version Appendix A may list additional
memory/storage-layer rules, but neither the present paper nor the
Albert work touches those layers.}
all target stack-layer algebraic simplification; the 7 rules that
overlap with our 83 are the algebraic-identity axioms (additive and
multiplicative identities, multiplicative absorption, double negation,
self-subtraction, absorption, etc.) that any SMT-verifiable bit-vector
peephole system almost inevitably contains, and they do not imply a
derivative dependency on the Albert rule set. Among the remaining 76
rules, 13 live at the x86 layer and 63 at the dMIR layer---covering the
multi-word carry-chain rules that emerge from decomposing the 256-bit
word, the \texttt{ADC}/\texttt{SBB} algebraic simplifications, and the
constant propagation and strength reduction tied to the JIT
code-generation path.

Runtime comparison-verification work---Breidenbach et al.\ at USENIX
Security 2018, the Hydra Framework~\cite{hydra_framework} (a different
system from the OOPSLA 2024 Hydra above, sharing only the name),
multi-client consensus-divergence detection systems, EVM fuzz testing,
and consensus test networks---detects divergence after deployment; the
present paper, in contrast, positions itself as static hardening during
development.

A complementary line of EVM-targeted research synthesizes higher-level
optimizations on the bytecode side. Max-SMT-based smart-contract
superoptimization~\cite{maxsmt_evm}, super-instruction
synthesis~\cite{vmil24_superinst}, neural-guided
exploration~\cite{ist25_neural_superopt}, and proof-producing
compilation for blockchain applications~\cite{jar25_proof_producing}
operate at the contract or bytecode-block level and are orthogonal to
the JIT IR/code-generation focus of this paper; translation
certification for smart contracts~\cite{scico24_translation_cert}
likewise targets a different abstraction boundary.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begingroup\sloppy\emergencystretch=3em
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.8em]
  \item \textbf{Trusted base.} The SMT check rests on the dMIR bit-vector
    semantic model and assumes its agreement with the runtime semantics
    of the actual JIT engine; systematic testing of that agreement is
    future work.
  \item \textbf{x86-layer verification coverage.} All 13 x86 rules are
    mechanical syntactic rewrites without a separate SMT check. Their
    correctness is covered by the paired baseline/enabled differential test runs of
    \S\ref{sec:correctness}; a formal model of x86 register and flag
    semantics is future work.
  \item \textbf{Workload coverage.} The speedup figures come from the
    27 evmone-bench benchmarks and do not represent all production
    contracts.
  \item \textbf{Scope of rule modeling.} Only the arithmetic and flag
    semantics before and after each peephole rewrite are checked for
    equivalence; gas accounting, memory aliasing, and storage-slot side
    effects are not modeled.
  \item \textbf{Dead-carry elimination is ineffective at the head of
    U256 add/sub chains.} At the head of a U256 add/sub chain, the
    third operand of the carry-propagating addition is a runtime value
    rather than a matchable zero constant, so the rule has no effect
    there by construction; the rule is kept because it remains
    effective on the independent carry-propagating additions inside the
    U256 multiplication lowering.
  \item \textbf{Measurement precision and compilation-overhead
    comparison.} \texttt{weierstrudel/15} and \texttt{signextend/one}
    fall within the $\pm 2\%$ noise band; \texttt{weierstrudel/1}
    stably slows down by \textbf{2.71\%}, and disabling each new rule
    in turn revealed no consistent negative contribution. The median
    compilation time of 0.45\,ms and the 95th percentile of 0.87\,ms per
    module are single-side measurements on the enabled build; the EVMC
    interface has no per-stage timing hook, a baseline comparison needs
    separate instrumentation, and this paper only reports an upper bound
    on the overall compilation latency.
  \item \textbf{Microbenchmark regression on the
    \texttt{memory\_grow\_mstore} family.} The 4 \texttt{mstore}
    variants slow down stably by \textbf{7.74\%}--\textbf{8.85\%} (CV
    below 2\% on both sides). A preliminary analysis localizes the
    regression to the matching behavior of the x86-layer
    compare-merging rule \texttt{optimizeCmp} on the memory-expand
    control-flow lowering path---that path lowers as a
    \texttt{SETCC}$\to$ zero-extend$\to$\texttt{TEST}$\to$\texttt{JCC}
    chain with the zero extension using \texttt{SUBREG\_TO\_REG}, which
    does not exactly match the compact chain covered by the current
    rule set; the exact root cause is left for future work. The 4
    \texttt{mload} variants of the same family do not expose this
    merging opportunity and accelerate by
    \textbf{+6.03\%}--\textbf{+7.26\%}.
\end{enumerate}
\endgroup
